# Week 7 - Lecture 4: Computer Misuse Act, governance and cyber bodies (MOD002647)

## Laws v ethics (L7 s3-4)
- Ethics: define socially acceptable behaviours.
- Laws: rules that mandate or prohibit certain behaviour; drawn from ethics.
- Main differences: laws carry the authority of a governing body; ethics come from moral attitudes or customs; ethics can be universal, laws tend to be local.

## Laws v policy (L7 s5)
- Policy: guidelines that describe acceptable and unacceptable behaviour; has penalties for non-compliance.
- Main differences: ignoring a policy is acceptable; ignoring a law is unacceptable.

## Business law (L7 s6)
- Due care: organisation makes sure employees know what is acceptable or unacceptable.
- Due diligence: organisation makes valid effort to protect employees, customers etc.

## Cybercrime (L7 s7) (Source: UK Government, 2016)
- Cybercrime describes two closely linked, but distinct criminal activities:
- Cyber-dependent crimes: crimes that can be committed only through the use of Information and Communications Technology (ICT) devices, where the devices are both the tool for committing the crime, and the target of the crime (e.g. developing and propagating malware for financial gain, hacking to steal, damage, distort or destroy data and/or network or activity).
- Cyber-enabled crimes: traditional crimes which can be increased in scale or reach by the use of computers, computer networks or other forms of ICT (such as cyber-enabled fraud and data theft).

## Cyber-dependent crimes (L7 s8) (Source: ICO, 2018)
- Illicit intrusion into computer networks, or downgrading or disrupting a service; e.g. hacking, defacing a website, DoS, malware.
- Laws listed: Computer Misuse Act 1990; Regulation of Investigation Powers Act 2000; Data Protection Act 1998.

## Cyber-enabled crimes (L7 s9) (Source: CPS, 2017)
- Crime transformed by technology:
  - economic crime - fraud, piracy, IP theft, forgery
  - online marketplaces for illegal items - drugs, guns, slaves
  - offensive communication - cyberbullying, social media
  - offenses against a specific target - cyberstalking
  - child sexual offences - illicit images, online grooming
  - extreme publications - pornography

## Computer Misuse Act 1990 - sections (L7 s10) (Source: UK Government, 1990)
- Section 1: UNAUTHORISED access to computer material.
- Section 2: UNAUTHORISED access with intent to commit or facilitate commission of further offences.
- Section 3: UNAUTHORISED acts with intent to impair, or with recklessness as to impairing, operation of computer.
- Section 3ZA: UNAUTHORISED acts causing, or creating risk of, serious damage.
- Section 3A: Making, supplying or obtaining articles for use in offence under section 1, 2, 3 or 3ZA.

## Hacking may also be an offence against (L7 s11)
- Computer Misuse Act 1990 [criminal]
- Data Protection Act 1998 [some civil + some criminal offences]
- Other legislation and/or contract of employment
- NB: often jurisdictional problems, especially cross border; e.g. long running saga of Gary McKinnon.

## CMA penalties (L7 s12)
- S1 offence: fine and/or 2 years prison [prev. 6 months] [now part of the scope of extradition treaties].
- S2 offence: fine and/or 5 years prison.
- S3 offence: fine and/or 10 years prison.
- (No penalty given on the slide for 3ZA or 3A.)

## Cybercriminal v cyber-hero (L7 s13)
- Hackers: break into computers or networks to gain access for various reasons.
- White hat attackers: break into networks or computer systems to discover weaknesses in order to improve the security of these systems.
- Gray hat attackers: somewhere between white and black hat attackers; may find a vulnerability and report it to the owners of the system if that action coincides with their agenda.
- Black hat attackers: unethical criminals who violate computer and network security for personal gain, or for malicious reasons, such as attacking networks.

## Penetration testing (L7 s14)
- An AUTHORISED person attempts to gain access to computer system/data (NB cannot 'PROVE' safe).
- Could be using hardware/software 'hacking'/social engineering or any mix.
- Only VERY limited need-to-know within organization.
- Easy to get arrested: needs authorisation letter from genuine high level manager who has the authority to give it (e.g. IT Director) AND present to verify.
- Important to use reputable organisation (e.g. your auditor?).

## Problems in prosecuting under CMA (L7 s15)
- proving access (S1) was unauthorized
- proving intent (S2) or malicious intent (S3)
- proof of offence (e.g. logs/audit trails) and that computer was working properly
- proof that defendant was the perpetrator; irrelevant tech. detail baffling judge or jury
- other Acts (e.g. Fraud Act 2006) may be easier to prove to criminal standard of proof.

## PCI DSS - Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (L7 s16-19)
- If you are a merchant of any size that accepts credit cards you must comply with PCI Security Council standards. (s16)
- PCI DSS was a result of collaboration between credit card companies (American Express, MasterCard, VISA, etc.). (s16)
- PCI DSS sets out ways to: protect card holder data; securely transfer card holder data; monitor your own network. (s16)
- Six goals (s17-18):
  1. Build and Maintain a Secure Network: install and maintain a firewall configuration to protect data; do not use vendor-supplied defaults for system passwords and other security parameters.
  2. Protect Cardholder Data: protect stored data (use encryption); encrypt transmission of cardholder data and sensitive information across public net.
  3. Maintain a Vulnerability Management Program: use and regularly update anti-virus software; develop and maintain secure systems and applications.
  4. Implement Strong Access Control Measures: restrict access to data by business need-to-know; assign a unique ID to each person with computer access; restrict physical access to cardholder data.
  5. Regularly Monitor and Test Networks: track and monitor all access to network resources and cardholder data; regularly test security systems and processes.
  6. Maintain an Information Security Policy: maintain a policy that addresses Information Security.
- Compliance (s19): compliant means you are doing your best to keep customer's data safe, regardless of if you a third party process it. If you lose card data you must declare it to ICO.

## Sarbanes Oxley (SOX) (L7 s20)
- US public companies must establish procedures ("hotlines") to enable employees anonymously to raise concerns about accounting or audit irregularities.
- Company directors must provide the correct information to auditors.
- EuroSOX: EU equivalent.

## Terms and conditions (L7 s21)
- T&Cs protect a company: fair use of the product; protect their copyright or licenses; outlines user expectations; outlines company expectations.
- T&Cs are legally binding. "How do you enforce them when no one reads them?"
- Herod's clause (Guardian, 2014) - public Wi-Fi example (reference title: "Londoners give up eldest children in public Wi-Fi security horror show").

## Professional bodies / organisations (L7 s22-25)
- National Cyber Crime Unit (NCCU) (s22): they are the police; part of the National Crime Agency (NCA); work with Regional Organised Crime Units (ROCUs). Job: respond to threats; collaborate with partners to reduce cyber crime. Report a cybercrime: www.actionfraud.police.uk.
- Government Communications Head Quarters (GCHQ) (s23): protect the UK (with law enforcement and other intelligence agencies); defend Government systems from cyber threat.
- National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) (s23): protect UK critical services from cyber attacks; manage major incidents; improve the security of the UK Internet.
- Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) (s24): independent authority; report a DPA/GDPR breach.
- National Institute for Standards and Technology (NIST) (s24): non-regulatory US agency; test and set standards for technology including security.
- Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP) (s25): global non-for-profit that supports application security; research, pen-testing; education.
- CREST (s25): non-for-profit organisation, representing the information security industry; research, pen-testing; education: "CyberEssentials" - professional qualifications.
